The problem

Hours spent building a slideshow no one asked for

Someone spends an afternoon every week piecing together a status update from memory, old chat messages, and whatever notes they can still find.

A problem tracker nobody actually looks at

A spreadsheet gets set up on day one to track what could go wrong, filled in once, and never opened again, right up until the thing it was supposed to catch happens.

Or a chatbot that just makes it up

Plenty of "AI" tools will cheerfully tell you a project is fine based on a vague prompt and a good vibe. If there isn't enough information to actually know, that's what it should say out loud, not something reassuring instead.

We didn't start from a standards binder

Most advice on running projects traces back to a handful of thick official playbooks that take a committee years to update. By the time one of them catches up to how teams actually work, most teams have already moved on to something else. So we skipped it. We didn't ask "what does the standard say"; we asked "what actually tells someone whether their work is really okay," and built that instead.

How it's different

The judgment call (is this actually fine, or not) is always made by plain, checkable rules first. Only after that's decided does anything get written into a sentence you'd read. A few real examples of how that plays out:

A deadline slips by a few days

That gets marked worth watching, not treated like a five-alarm fire. There's a little room built in before a late date actually becomes a real problem, because that's how someone experienced would judge it too.

Not enough information yet

If there hasn't been enough recent activity to say for sure how things are going, it says exactly that: not enough data, instead of guessing everything's fine just because nothing bad turned up.

A written update you can actually trust

Before any message about a real problem goes out, it's checked against the actual facts first. Get that check wrong, and the message doesn't go out. It's never quietly fixed for you, and never sent out downplaying something real.

You don't need PM experience to use this

A step-by-step Getting Started checklist walks a first-time user through starting a project, adding tasks, and running the first status check, and it tailors its suggestions to who you actually are, whether that's a founder wearing every hat, someone just handed a project to manage, or a PM who's run dozens of these before. Stuck on anything? A built-in Help assistant answers questions about using the product itself, in plain language, any time you ask.

What it does

Ten focused helpers, each good at one thing, doing the work that would otherwise mean hiring more people just to keep up. Turn on only the ones you actually need. You pay per helper, never for a bundle of stuff you don't use.

Getting started

Has a real back-and-forth conversation with whoever's requesting the work to understand what's actually needed, asking a follow-up when an answer is vague, and speaking up when two answers don't add up, instead of just writing down whatever it's told.

Turning it into tasks

Takes what's been agreed on and breaks it into a clear, ordered task list, keeping that list current as new requests come in, with the order set by real, visible rules, not a gut feeling.

Status checks

Looks at real tasks, deadlines, and messages and tells you plainly whether a project is on track, worth watching, or behind, and points to exactly what led to that answer, so you can check it yourself.

Spotting trouble early

Notices problems before they become a fire drill: work stuck behind one holdup, tasks with no one clearly responsible for them, and warning phrases like "we'll deal with that later" buried in updates.

Writing updates

Drafts a status message in whatever tone fits, whether that's a quick internal note, a client-facing update, an executive summary, or an urgent heads-up, all without ever softening or leaving out a real problem.

The big picture

Rolls every active project into one view, so a leader can see which ones need attention first, without sitting through five separate status meetings to find out.

Keeping tasks current

Reads real evidence in task history and messages and proposes updates to task details, applied automatically if you want, or held for your okay first if you'd rather keep the final say.

Regular summaries

Sends a plain-language recap of what's changed daily, weekly, or monthly, so nobody has to piece one together by hand.

Catching new requests

Watches incoming messages (a forwarded email, a chat excerpt, meeting notes) for signs that someone's actually asking for a new project, and flags it for a person to look at.

Learning from what happened

When a project wraps up, writes a short, specific list of what worked and what to do differently next time, the kind of thing you'd actually tell the next person taking on something similar, not generic advice.

Two more things come free with every plan: something that quietly coordinates all of the above behind the scenes, and a built-in help assistant for using the product itself.
